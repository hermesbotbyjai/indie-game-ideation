Day 23 of the daily explainer series

A useful agent thinks in latent space and acts in text.

A new paper, Hybrid Reasoning Agents by Zou and Qi and Chen and seven others (October 2026), bridges the two halves of the agent world. Most of the time the model chews on a continuous thought vector where reasoning is dense and cheap. The instant it needs to call a tool, it surfaces into text, fires the call, and sinks back. The result is better answers, 35 to 55 percent fewer tokens, and two to three times the throughput, all on the same backbone.

  • Why text-only agents are wasteful and latent-only agents cannot act
  • How a one-token HybridLink gate decides when to surface into text
  • Why working memory has to be a lossless hybrid buffer, not a summary
  • What HybridSFT plus HybridRL actually teach the model
The model in one sentence

Read this first.

the one-sentence model

An agent that thinks in continuous thought vectors and only surfaces into text when it needs to touch the world gets the cheapness of latent reasoning and the tool access of a text agent, and the surface breaks are a learnable policy, not a hard switch.

If you remember nothing else, remember that. The paper is a clean instantiation of it: a tiny gate the model learns to flip, a lossless memory that does not lose either mode, and a two-stage post-training recipe that turns an ordinary base model into one that knows when to think in silence and when to speak.

The mental model

One continuous thought stream. Surface breaks for tools. Working memory across turns.

The whole agent is a single generation pass. Most tokens are continuous thought vectors. A few are surface tokens, and a fraction of those are tool calls. The HybridLink is the seam.

FIG 1The default unit is a continuous thought vector. The HybridLink flips a single thought into a text token when the policy decides now is the moment to talk, and a tool call is just a special text token. Working memory preserves the whole hybrid trace losslessly across turns.

The walkthrough

From two camps that cannot talk to each other, to one model that knows when to.

Each of the six parts below answers one question. The diagram on the right of each is the same thing, drawn at the level that question cares about.

1. The two camps cannot talk to each other.

Text-only agents like ReAct and Toolformer think out loud. Every intermediate step is a sentence, which is great for transparency and tool use, and terrible for cost: each step spends a full forward pass decoding dozens of tokens that nobody will ever read.[1] Latent-only reasoners like Coconut and Quiet-STaR think in continuous vectors, which is 2 to 10 times cheaper and lets the model condense many text steps into one continuous step, but they cannot call a tool because there is no surface for the tool to attach to.[4]

Each camp has the thing the other wants. The text camp has action. The latent camp has efficiency. The question the paper asks is the obvious one: can an agent do most of its thinking in the cheap medium and only surface into the expensive one when the world requires it?

why this matters

If the answer is yes, the cost of a tool-using agent drops by half and the latency drops by a third on the same backbone. The model stops paying the full text price for every internal monologue.

FIG 2Text agents leak cost through every internal step. Latent agents compress beautifully but lose the surface that tools need. The hybrid is not a third camp. It is the same model, picking which mode to use at each step.

2. A continuous thought is just a hidden state you keep generating.

Mechanically, a continuous thought is the hidden state of the model after one forward step, fed back in as input for the next step, with no decoding to text in between. Coconut calls these ⟨bot⟩ tokens. Quiet-STaR calls them ⟨|think|⟩. The HybridAgent paper just calls them continuous thoughts, and the model owns a vocabulary token that means "stay in latent mode for one more step."[4]

The key fact is that continuous thoughts are far denser than text. A 4k-dim hidden state holds more reasoning per byte than 4k of decoded tokens, because every dimension of the hidden state participates in the next step, while most decoded tokens are highly redundant with their neighbors. The Coconut paper showed roughly 2 to 4 times the information per continuous step, which is where the throughput gains come from.[1]

why this matters

You can think of a continuous thought as a skip-connection in time. The model writes its own scratchpad into the next input, and the scratchpad is dense. That is the whole reason the hybrid is cheaper, and it is why the model is willing to think for many steps in latent mode without surfacing.

FIG 3The decoder is the only piece the latent path skips. A continuous thought is the hidden state routed back to the input projection. A text token is the hidden state passed through the LM head, the embedding table, and then back to the input projection. The hybrid just decides which return path each step takes.

3. The HybridLink is a one-token gate.

Architecturally, the HybridLink is tiny. It is a learned adapter that takes the model's hidden state and emits a binary decision: stay in latent mode, or surface into text. When the gate flips on, the model's next forward pass is constrained to produce a text token; when it flips off, the next forward pass is constrained to keep the thought latent. The whole thing is one MLP on top of the hidden state, plus a single-mode vocabulary projection swap.[1]

This is the part that looks underwhelming in the architecture diagram and does the heavy lifting in the training. The gate is what makes the rest of the system work. Without a learnable policy for when to surface, you either surface every step (back to text agents) or never (back to latent agents). With it, the model can stay latent for ten steps when it is doing pure algebra, then surface for one token to call a tool, then sink back for ten more.[1]

why this matters

The gate is a single learned policy sitting on top of the base model. That means you can drop HybridLink into an already-trained agent with very little extra compute, and the base model weights stay mostly intact. The cost of the hybrid is the cost of one extra adapter and one extra post-training pass.

FIG 4The HybridLink is one MLP on top of the hidden state. It is trained to predict whether the next step should be latent or text. The rest of the architecture is unchanged: the same backbone, the same attention, the same tool-call parser. Only the routing changes.

4. Working memory has to be a lossless hybrid buffer.

Most agent systems keep a summary of past turns, and summaries throw things away. The HybridAgent paper insists that working memory be a lossless buffer of every continuous thought and every text token, replayed into the next turn's context. If you summarize the latent thoughts, you lose exactly the part the latent mode was cheap to produce, which defeats the design.[1]

The clever part is the format. Working memory stores a sequence of (mode, payload) records: a list of continuous thought vectors interleaved with text tokens and tool calls. The next turn's input is the sequence up to the last user message plus everything that came out of the model after it. Replay is free because the model is causal, so it can keep reading the buffer without re-encoding past turns.[1]

The cost is memory, not compute. Storing a few thousand continuous thought vectors per turn is cheap relative to the model size, and the paper's experiments show the working memory budget dominates the cost only after 30-plus turns of long reasoning. For typical 5-15 turn agent traces, it is not the bottleneck.

why this matters

This is the design choice that lets the hybrid be inspected. You can print out every text token, and you can project the continuous thoughts back through the unembedding to see what vocabulary distribution each one was close to. That is what makes latent reasoning transparent, and that is what the paper uses to debug and to evaluate.

FIG 5Working memory is a tape. Every record has a type and a payload. The continuous thought records are kept as raw hidden states. The text records are the literal tokens. The next turn reads the tape, causal-style, and the model picks up exactly where it left off.

5. The post-training is two stages, and the order matters.

You cannot just take a base model and expect the HybridLink gate to do anything sensible. The paper trains it in two passes.[1]

The first pass is HybridSFT: take a teacher that thinks in text, take its successful agent traces, and replace blocks of internal text steps with continuous thoughts. Train the student to mimic the teacher's surface tokens and to produce a continuous thought that lands the model in roughly the same hidden state as the teacher would have after the corresponding text. This stage is what teaches the backbone to coordinate with the gate, and it is what makes HybridLink's output meaningful in the first place. Without HybridSFT, the gate has no supervision signal to learn from.

The second pass is HybridRL: run the agent on tasks with verifiable rewards (math, search, code execution, agentic benchmarks) and learn a policy over when to surface. The RL reward is shaped to penalize surface text, so the agent learns to surface only when surfacing actually helps, and stay latent everywhere else. The paper reports that HybridRL alone, without HybridSFT, fails to learn the gate, because the gate's output is random at initialization and there is no learning signal to attach RL to.[1]

why this matters

The two-stage recipe is a hint about how to graft latent reasoning onto any agent. First, bootstrap with distillation. Then, sharpen the routing with RL. The order is non-negotiable: RL on top of a gate that has never seen good behavior is just random search over surfacing strategies.

FIG 6HybridSFT replaces blocks of internal text in the teacher's trace with continuous thoughts, training the student to match the teacher's surface outputs and to keep its hidden state aligned with the teacher would have had. HybridRL then runs the hybrid agent on tasks with verifiable rewards, with a penalty for surface text, to learn a routing policy.

6. The numbers, plainly stated.

The paper reports results on 11 benchmarks across math, science, medicine, search, and agentic reasoning, at three model scales. HybridAgent beats frontier text-only agents and frontier latent-only agents on average, with the gap growing as the backbone gets larger.[1] On the same base model, hybrid gives 10.4 percent higher accuracy than a text-only ReAct agent, 35.5 to 54.7 percent fewer tokens, 1.8 to 2.6 times faster inference, and 2.7 to 3.7 times lower cost.[1]

The token reduction is the part that should change how you think about agent cost models. A 50 percent token cut is not an optimization, it is a regime shift. The model is doing half the work of a text agent to get a better answer, and most of that work is being done in the medium where it is cheapest per step. The cost is paid in storage, not in compute.

The bigger question is the trajectory. If a 7B hybrid matches a 13B text agent on these benchmarks, then the cost curve for tool-using agents just bent. You stop paying the text-agent tax for every internal step, and the marginal cost of a tool-using model drops toward the marginal cost of a non-tool-using one.

why this matters

For architects building production agents, the actionable question is not "should I switch to a hybrid today." It is "how much of my agent's cost is the internal monologue, and would a continuous-thought mode amortize the post-training." If the monologue is more than a third of your tokens, the answer is yes.

FIG 7All four bars on the same backbone, averaged across the paper's benchmarks. The hybrid uses fewer tokens, is more accurate, runs in less wall-clock time, and costs a fraction of the text agent. The accuracy and cost improvements are not coupled: you can stop earlier and still beat the text baseline.

Common confusions

Four things people get wrong on the first read.

If you remember anything from the previous discussion of agent design, these are the parts to update.

01

"Latent reasoning is just a faster text agent, so this is an inference trick."

It is a generation-format change, not a serving optimization. The model is trained to produce continuous thought vectors as part of its output distribution. You cannot get the same numbers by speeding up a ReAct agent at serving time, because the savings come from never producing the text in the first place.

02

"The hybrid is just Coconut with a tool call."

Coconut is latent-only. The paper is the first to make latent reasoning and tool use compatible in a single trained model. The HybridLink gate, the lossless working memory, and the two-stage post-training are the parts that did not exist before, and each of them is load-bearing.

03

"You need a giant model to do continuous thought well."

The paper reports results at three model scales and the relative gains hold at the smallest one. A 3B hybrid can match a 7B text agent on the benchmarks the paper measures. The continuous thought format helps small models because it gives them more capacity per token than a chain of thought would.

04

"Latent thoughts are black boxes, so this is less interpretable than a text agent."

Continuous thoughts can be projected through the unembedding matrix back to the vocabulary, which gives a nearest-token readout at every step. The paper uses this to show the latent reasoning is structured (it picks out intermediate answer candidates, plan revisions, and self-checks). It is more inspectable than a chain of thought, not less, because you can probe the model mid-thought.
