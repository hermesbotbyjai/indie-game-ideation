Day 20 · Mechanistic Interpretability

Neural networks are already clean.

The canonical evidence of polysemanticity was misread. What's left is one learning principle, ordinary generalization at fractal scale, and a network you can read.

  • Why the canonical "cat-and-car neuron" actually detects a V shape.
  • Recursive generalization as the real second learning principle.
  • Why 99% of late-layer neurons in InceptionV1 are now legible.
  • What this means for mechanistic interpretability at trillion-parameter scale.

Walkthrough

Five steps from knotted to legible.

The essay's argument is built like a proof with one move per beat. Each move is small. Together they change what's possible.

1. The canonical polysemantic neuron wasn't a tangle.

InceptionV1's 4e:55 is famous for firing on cat faces and car fronts. Cammarata opens the same image panel he's seen a hundred times and asks the question no one asked for a decade: what one thing might this be? The answer is a V shape. Snouts, fox faces, fireboat jets and pointed glasses come from a single shared pattern. The neurons we thought were arbitrary storage were a single pattern stretched across nouns.

The negative pole makes it obvious: rounded things push it down. That's not how arbitrary mixtures look. Arbitrary mixtures don't have a coherent opposite.

why this mattersEvery claim of polysemanticity you've ever read is a claim about which microscope was used to look. If the same neuron renders as clean under better tools, the tangle was in the tool, not the network.

FIG 2 The "V neuron" redrawn as what it actually detects. The pointed shapes drive it; the round ones push it down.

2. The superposition circuit wasn't superposition either.

The second pillar of the knotted-network story was the 2019 car-into-dogs circuit. It looked like the network hid a car feature among unrelated dog neurons to be read out downstream. Cammarata shows that wasn't happening. Cars and dogs were grouped because of a shared pose. InceptionV1 has more than a hundred pose neurons, and no one had noticed. The car was differentiating, not hiding.

Part of the confusion was feature visualization reward hacking: in deep layers, the optimizer falls into dog neurons because there are many dogs in the training data and never recovers. Render through the car path instead, and the apparent dogs turn back into cars.

why this mattersThe canonical evidence for superposition in vision models dissolves when you visualize correctly. This is the difference between "we don't understand the network" and "we don't understand our microscope."

FIG 3 Same neurons, two render paths. The dogs above and the cars below are the same downstream neurons; only the visualization through-path differs.

3. Recursive generalization, not superposition.

The remaining apparent polysemanticity follows a motif Cammarata calls recursive generalization. A neuron starts by detecting "iy". It grows to detect "diy". It grows again to detect "do it yourself", which doesn't contain "iy" at all. The branching mirrors a tree. When you follow the least legible neurons in InceptionV1's deep layers, they aren't folding unrelated things; they're sprouting adjacent generalizations.

The InceptionV1 late-layer mess was a learning failure of one principle, not a switch to a second. Architectural fixes that let deep layers learn better (the same ones that helped residual networks) made those layers more legible as a side effect. Same weights, same training data, better deep layers, and 99 to 100% of late-layer neurons become interpretable.

why this mattersThere is one learning principle, generalization at fractal scale. The field bet on a second one, superposition, and the bet looks wrong. That changes the foundation of mechanistic interpretability, not its tooling.

FIG 4 A neuron that started as "iy" and ended as "do it yourself." Each branch is one generalization step; nothing was folded.

4. Modern language models are cleaner than the rubric turned.

In 2023, Anthropic's Towards Monosemanticity gave the median neuron in a small language model a 0 out of 14 on its rubric. OpenAI's automated interpretability run found about 1.7% of neurons interpretable. Cammarata's preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B estimate around 90% of neuron poles have one main job, with another 7% having two related jobs, and 2 to 4% not yet understood. The rubric didn't get easier; the network is cleaner, and the tools are better.

When you read the neuron right, most of what looked like polysemanticity turns out to be the model detecting subtle patterns humans and binary languages alike keep missing. The "Titanic neuron" also fires on steam engines. The "Trump neuron" also fires on MAGA hats. These aren't arbitrary. They are routine related events.

why this mattersIf 90% of late neurons in a frontier model are interpretable, mechanistic interpretability is a tractable engineering target, not a moonshot. That changes how you staff alignment teams.

FIG 5 The cleanliness bar moved because the rubric and the microscope moved, not because the networks got simpler. Modern LMs are easier to read than older small models.

5. A bitter lesson for interpretability.

Cammarick frames it as the bitter lesson in miniature. The simple thing at absurd scale beats the clever one. Most of the field's decade of work built clever decompositions (sparse autoencoders, transcoders, dictionary learning) because the network seemed knotted. If the network is mostly clean, ordinary investigation with good tools scales better than another decomposition objective.

This is highly speculative and he says so. But the evidence so far: the same kind of normal-science workflow that worked on InceptionV1 works on Qwen3-32B and Kimi K3. Trillion-parameter models aren't close to solved due to size, but the trajectory is favorable, not unfavorable.

why this mattersFor architects, this is a strategic signal. If you build an interpretability stack today, lean on agents that run experiments at scale rather than on training another decomposition. The compounding rate of return is higher when the map fills in.

Common confusions

Four things people get wrong.

If you remember anything from the previous decade of interpretability, these are the parts to update.

01

"Polysemanticity proves networks are arbitrary."

Polysemanticity was a property of small and noisy tools applied to mid-layer vision models. Modern tools on frontier LMs find 90%+ of poles with one main job. The arbitrariness was the rubric, not the network.

02

"Superposition is a second learning principle."

It's a misread of recursive generalization. The remaining apparent mixture is mostly the same principle, generalization, at fractal scale. There is one learning principle, not two.

03

"To study a network, first decompose it."

The decomposition-first path assumes the network is knotted. If the network is mostly clean, decomposition hides the very errors you need to fix. Studying the network directly is faster and points straight at broken tools.

04

"Neurons are noun detectors."

Most neurons are better described as verbs. A neuron that fires on a pile of unrelated commas is detecting a feeling the model summarized at the punctuation. Asking "what noun does this detect?" mislabels most of what neurons do.
