Day 3 — Inference · KV cache · Attention

Multi-Head Latent Attention: one latent vector per token, not per head.

The single trick that took DeepSeek from a 93% KV cache cut (V2) to a 437x cut (V4.1-Flash), and let them ship a 1M-context MoE without melting your HBM.

Why MQA and GQA only get you so far The low-rank KV joint compression math How MLA coexists with position info (decoupled RoPE) The 437x story from V1 → V4.1-Flash
The model in one sentence

Standard attention stores a separate K and V vector for every head and every past token, while MLA compresses both into a single shared low-rank latent per token and reconstructs the per-head K and V on the fly during attention.

Mental model

The whole thing in one picture.

Every token carries one small ct through the cache. The full per-head K, V matrices are computed on the fly from that latent plus the token's input. The cache footprint shrinks to whatever fits in ct.

Figure 1 — Cache footprint per token. MHA: O(nh·dh) per layer. MLA: O(dc) per layer, with the per-head K and V rebuilt at attention time from the latent.

Walkthrough

Six pieces, each one idea.

1. Why the KV cache is the bottleneck.

During autoregressive decode, the model reuses the K and V tensors of every past token to compute attention for the new one. Caching them avoids a quadratic recompute, but the cache itself grows linearly with sequence length. For a 70B model at 128K context in fp16, the cache alone needs roughly 2 × 80 layers × 128K tokens × 128 heads × 128 dim × 2 bytes ≈ 80 GiB per request — and that number is independent of how short or long the response actually is.

The cost shows up in three places. HBM capacity caps how many requests you can batch. Memory bandwidth bottlenecks attention compute because every token reads every past K and V. And for long-running agent sessions, SSD and host RAM fill up with persistent prefix caches. KV cache is the reason a 1M-context model is hard, not the parameters.

Figure 2 — Cache size per request scales linearly with sequence length. The ceiling stops being parameters and starts being HBM.

why this matters Any architectural trick that makes K and V smaller per token compounds across layers, across batched requests, and across long contexts. A 10x cache reduction means 10x more requests fit in HBM, and that is what turns a research demo into a deployment.

2. The earlier tricks (MQA, GQA) and why they plateau.

Two prior moves push the same lever. Multi-Query Attention (MQA) has every head share a single K and V. That cuts the cache by a factor of nh, but quality drops noticeably because the heads can no longer attend to different things. Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) compromises: group the heads, share K/V within a group. Llama-3 uses 8 KV heads behind 32 query heads — a 4x cut, acceptable quality. GQA is now the default in most modern decoders.

But GQA only addresses the head axis. The per-head dimension dh stays untouched, and there is a quality ceiling: enough groups to be expressive, few enough to actually save memory. That is where MLA enters: it does not reduce the number of K/V heads, it reduces what is stored per head.

Figure 3 — MLA is not "fewer heads." It is "the same number of effective heads, but the heads are computed from a single compressed vector at attention time."

why this matters MQA and GQA are lossy: sharing K and V across heads forces every head to look at the same evidence. MLA compresses, not shares. The full per-head K and V are still computable, just not stored. Quality ends up closer to MHA than to GQA, with cache size closer to MQA.

3. The compression math.

The mechanism is one linear projection. Given input ht for token t, MLA computes a single low-rank latent:

ct = WDKV ht ∈ ℝdc

The full per-head keys and values are then reconstructed on the fly:

kCt = WUK ct    vt = WUV ct

What gets cached during decode is the single vector ct per token, not the nh × dh per-head K and V. With dc chosen around dh or a few times dh, the cache footprint drops by a factor of roughly (2 · nh · dh) / dc — and the per-head tensors are still produced at every step, just not stored.

Figure 4 — One down-projection into the cache. Two up-projections compute per-head K and V at attention time.

why this matters The reconstruction adds two matmuls per layer per step. For a 70B model on an H100, those matmuls are free next to the attention GEMM that follows. The cache size win is what dominates serving cost.

4. Decoupled RoPE: how MLA keeps position information.

Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) get applied to K inside the attention dot product, so K must carry positional info. If you cache a compressed latent and only reconstruct K later, you have two choices. Either RoPE the reconstructed K (cheap but breaks absorbed-weight optimizations), or carry an additional small per-head key kRt alongside the latent and RoPE only that. MLA picks the second.

In practice, the cache for each token stores: one ct of size dc ≈ 4·dh ≈ 512 for a 70B model, and one tiny kRt of size dRh ≈ 64. The attention kernel computes RoPE on the fly for both the reconstructed query qCt and the cached kRt, and merges them into the final per-head key. The cost of preserving position information is one extra ~64-dim vector per token, which is essentially free.

Figure 5 — The decoupled RoPE design lets MLA keep positional attention without giving up the compression win.

why this matters Early attempts at compressing K and V broke because they broke RoPE. Decoupled RoPE was the unlock. Every modern MLA-style design (TransMLA, MiniMax MLA variants) follows this exact pattern: compress content, keep a tiny dedicated position channel.

5. From V2 (93% cut) to V4.1-Flash (437x cut).

MLA has compounded with four other tricks across DeepSeek's generations. Each one attacks a different axis of the cache footprint, and they multiply.

Figure 6 — MLA bought 15x. fp8, CSA, and CED architecture bought the rest. The 437x is multiplicative, not magical.

The compounding matters. MLA cuts the per-token content by ~15x. fp8 KV cache halves the byte width. Compressed Sparse Attention (CSA) in V4 groups every 8 or 128 tokens into one compressed slot, with an indexer that picks which slot each query attends to. And FP4 KV in V4.1-Flash halves the byte width again via E2M1 with per-16-channel scaling. Stack four 5-10x factors and you get hundreds.

why this matters This is why "DeepSeek shipped a 1M-context MoE for cheap" is not an accident. It is the same compression trick applied four times along different axes. The lesson: pick the cheapest axis to compress next, and never let quantization eat accuracy on its own without architectural backup.

6. Where MLA is used today.

MLA is not exotic anymore. DeepSeek-V2, V3, V3.1, V3.2, V4, and V4.1-Flash all use it. MiniMax-M3 uses an MLA-style compressed latent with decoupled RoPE. TransMLA is a published conversion procedure that takes a pre-trained GQA model and turns it into MLA without retraining from scratch. FlashMLA is the open-source CUDA kernel from DeepSeek that fuses RoPE, attention, RoPE, and cast into a single pass for MLA-shaped inputs — and it is the kernel shipping in vLLM's production builds for MLA models.

Figure 7 — From research paper to production kernel in roughly two years. MLA is now table stakes for long-context serving.

why this matters If you are designing or evaluating an attention mechanism, MLA is the benchmark. Anything you build should at minimum match its cache-to-quality ratio, or beat it on one axis (latency, training cost, ease of quantization) while matching on the others. The bar has moved.
Common confusions

Four things people get wrong.

01

"MLA is just MQA with more groups."

Wrong. MQA shares the same K and V across all heads, which is a memory win but a quality loss. MLA compresses the KV representation into a low-rank latent and reconstructs the full per-head tensors at attention time. The cache holds one vector, not one shared head.

02

"MLA breaks RoPE, so you cannot use it."

The naive version does. The actual MLA design uses a small decoupled RoPE key kR kept alongside the latent, so position information is preserved without storing full positional keys. The kernels (FlashMLA) fuse the RoPE into the attention GEMM with one extra small vector.

03

"The 437x cache cut is just MLA."

No — MLA gave ~93% of the way there. The DeepSeek lineage added fp8 quantization (2x), Compressed Sparse Attention across tokens (4 to 128x), FP4 KV in V4.1 (another 2x), and CED architecture for persistent caching (another 2x). The 437x is multiplicative across four orthogonal axes.

04

"MLA is slower per step because of the reconstruction."

In theory yes — you pay two extra matmuls. In practice no — the attention GEMM becomes much cheaper because the per-head K and V are smaller to read from memory, and bandwidth dominates over arithmetic. FlashMLA shows throughput roughly matching or beating standard MHA at the same parameter count.
