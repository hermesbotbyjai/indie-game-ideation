Diffusion Language Models

A new generation paradigm that fills in many tokens at once by iteratively denoising a fully masked sequence, with bidirectional context, instead of committing to one next token at a time.

How a DLM turns “all masks” into a coherent paragraph in 8 to 16 steps.

THE MODEL IN ONE SENTENCE
Mental model A diffusion language model is a Transformer trained to take a fully masked sequence and iteratively fill in the most confident tokens at each step, with every other token visible as context, until nothing is masked.
THE MENTAL MODEL

One image: a paragraph being uncovered.

A DLM does not write left to right. It imagines the whole paragraph as a slot machine, asks the model which slots look most certain, locks those in, and re-asks on what is left. Below, watch the sequence go from all masks to a clean sentence in eight steps.

Each row is one model call. The model sees every slot, predicts a distribution for every mask, and locks the most confident positions. The confidence bar below tracks how much of the sequence is settled.

WALKTHROUGH

Six ideas, in order, that together explain a DLM.

Each section gives you one mechanism, one diagram, and the practical reason it matters when you read a paper or pick an architecture.

1. Training looks like BERT, but over the whole noise schedule.

A DLM is trained by sampling a noise level t in [0, 1], masking that fraction of tokens in a clean sequence, and asking the Transformer to predict the originals. The masking ratio is random each step, not a fixed 15%, so the model sees everything from one mask to nearly all masks. The loss is a cross-entropy over the masked positions only, summed across the noise schedule. This is BERT's objective, but the random t is the trick that turns it into a generative model: at inference the model can be started at t=1 (all masks) and stepped down to t=0 (no masks).

Three samples from the noise schedule the model trains on. Each example is a different t.

Why this matters: the random t is what gives DLMs their flexibility. A model trained only at t=0.15 (vanilla BERT) cannot start from all-masks and walk down. LLaDA and iLLaDA both keep this schedule, only changing architecture and recipe.

2. Bidirectional attention is the deal-breaker versus AR.

An autoregressive Transformer uses a causal mask so position i can only attend to positions ≤ i. A DLM drops the causal mask. Every token, whether real or mask, attends to every other token. That is the single architectural change that makes denoising possible: when half the sequence is still masks, the model needs the surviving tokens to inform every missing one, and it needs to do so without leaking information about the answer. Without bidirectional attention, the model cannot fill in non-contiguous spans coherently.

Light cells = positions allowed to attend. AR is a triangle; DLM is a solid square.

Why this matters: the causal mask in AR is what makes next-token prediction well-defined. Drop it, and you have to invent a different training objective. The masked-token objective is that invention. The square mask is also why DLMs need extra care to avoid answer leakage: every predicted token is conditioned on every other.

3. Decoding picks high-confidence positions, then re-asks.

At inference, start with all masks. Run the Transformer once. It outputs a logit distribution for every masked position. Rank the confidences, fill in the top fraction (say 12.5% of remaining masks), re-mask the rest, run again. The standard schedule is linear: if you want N tokens in K steps, you reveal roughly (current mask count) / (K − current step) tokens each pass. The result is parallel: every locked-in token was decided in the same forward pass as the next one.

The reveal order is content-dependent. The model often locks in low-information words first.

Why this matters: because steps are parallel, K=8 denoising passes for 64 tokens cost the same wall time as K=8 AR decoding steps for 8 tokens. The throughput win compounds with sequence length. Mercury and Inception report 5 to 10x wall-clock speedups at comparable quality.

4. AR and DLM have the same total compute, but spend it differently.

An AR model with N tokens does N forward passes, one per token, each with a growing KV cache. A DLM with the same N tokens does K forward passes (K ≪ N), each with full bidirectional attention across all positions. The DLM is heavier per step because attention is quadratic in sequence length and there is no causal restriction, but it has 1/K-th as many steps. The crossover is somewhere around K = 16: for a 64-token answer, eight bidirectional passes match sixty-four causal passes on modern accelerators because the matrix multiplies dominate and the kernel launch overhead amortizes.

AR is a long thin row. DLM is a short fat block. Both fill the same total area.

Why this matters: papers that claim DLM speedups do not mean cheaper training. They mean fewer decoder passes for the same answer length, which compounds with the parallelism of each pass. The training cost is similar to AR pretraining of the same size.

5. Infilling, reversal, and any-order generation fall out for free.

Because the model sees the entire sequence as context, you can fix any subset of positions to values and ask it to fill the rest. Prompt infill, code completion in the middle of a file, paraphrasing a chunked document, all of these are the same inference call. AR models handle them by stalling: they have to either truncate the prompt or generate left to right around a fixed anchor. LLaDA 8B beats GPT-4o on a reversal poem completion task because it can output the answer in any order, which AR fundamentally cannot.

Gray tokens are fixed prompt. Green is what the model fills in. Order is content-driven.

Why this matters: infill quality is the one place DLMs have a real architectural edge, not a training-recipe edge. If your product is “edit this paragraph” or “complete the middle of this function,” a DLM is a better fit than an AR model with hacky infill tricks.

6. The 2026 recipe: iLLaDA, AURORA-LM, and block diffusion.

The current frontier is convergence. iLLaDA (Sep 2026) keeps LLaDA's masked discrete objective and scales it to 8B parameters on 12T tokens, getting 14 to 21 point improvements on math and code benchmarks and remaining competitive with Qwen2.5 7B. AURORA-LM (Sep 2026) abandons discrete token masks and instead trains a diffusion model in a continuous latent space defined by a text autoencoder, then decodes back to tokens through a fixed decoder. Set Diffusion (Jul 2026) factorizes the likelihood over arbitrary token sets so the model can decode in any order with KV cache updates after every step. They are not the same model. They share the same paradigm: fill in a sequence in parallel, re-ask, lock, repeat.

Four recipes. Same paradigm. Different bottlenecks, different best-fit workloads.

Why this matters: the field has not picked a winner. If you are picking an architecture for your own system in late 2026, you are choosing between discrete-token DLMs (easiest to serve, integrates with existing tokenizers), block-diffusion hybrids (best KV-cache story), and continuous-latent DLMs (best quality ceiling, hardest to deploy). Read the deployment story before the benchmark table.
COMMON CONFUSIONS

Four things people get wrong about DLMs.

CONFUSION 01

A DLM is just BERT with extra steps.

No. BERT trains at one fixed mask ratio (15%) and is used as an encoder, not a generator. A DLM trains across the full noise schedule from 0 to 1, and the model is designed to start from all-masks and walk down. The training objective looks similar because it is similar; the inference procedure and the schedule are what make it generative.

CONFUSION 02

DLMs are faster because they use less compute.

No. Total compute is roughly matched. They are faster because they amortize the cost of a forward pass across many tokens. K parallel steps is cheaper on a modern GPU than K sequential steps with KV cache growth, because matrix multiplies dominate and the kernel launch overhead is paid K times instead of N times.

CONFUSION 03

DLMs beat AR on every benchmark now.

No, and not even close. iLLaDA-Base is competitive with Qwen2.5 7B Base, and iLLaDA-Instruct still trails Qwen2.5 7B Instruct. The instruction-tuned gap is the open problem. DLMs are also worse on long-form reasoning and chain-of-thought, where the iterative refinement advantage does not yet translate.

CONFUSION 04

A DLM can run on any pretrained AR model with no retraining.

No. Bidirectional attention is incompatible with a causal checkpoint. You either retrain from scratch (LLaDA, iLLaDA) or distill from a teacher (Dream finetunes Qwen2.5). The Introspective Diffusion work tries to keep a causal base model usable, but it pays a quality cost and is still research-stage.
