Day 1 · Embeddings

Gemma Embeddings

How Google's open embedding model turns text into numbers, and why those numbers are useful for retrieval, search, clustering, and just about anything you'd want a model to compare.

Start from zero Visual mental model Senior DS depth One readable page
The model in one sentence

"A Gemma embedding is a learned function f(text) → R768 that turns any string of words into a fixed-length vector of numbers, where semantically similar inputs land near each other in space, and dissimilar inputs land far apart.

The mental model

The whole pipeline, end to end

One sentence in. One vector out. Watch the pieces wire up.

Same 768 numbers, every time, for any input up to the model's context length. Comparable across sentences, paragraphs, or whole documents, the way integers are comparable across any list.

Walkthrough

Six pieces, one each

Each section below has one paragraph of explanation, one inline diagram, and one short aside on why this piece earns its keep.

A · What it actually is

An embedding is a vector where meaning is geometry.

Take the words king, queen, car, and banana. Each one becomes a list of numbers. The numbers themselves look like noise, but if you plotted them in space, royalty would cluster together. Vehicles would sit in another neighborhood. Fruit would be somewhere else entirely. The whole job of an embedding model is to learn that geometry so you can use it later, for search, clustering, classification, or anything that needs a notion of close.

How close is close? You measure it with cosine similarity, which we'll come back to. For now, just remember: similarity in vector space is the substitute for similarity in meaning.

A 2D sketch. Real Gemma vectors live in 768 dimensions, which is enough room for words to find their own neighborhoods without bumping into each other.

Why this matters

Everything downstream rides on the geometry. Retrieval, deduplication, recommendation, classification, clustering, anomaly detection. None of these work unless the embedding function learned distances that match what humans would call similar.

B · Tokenization

First, text becomes tokens, then token IDs.

Models don't read characters or words. They read tokens, which are short chunks of characters picked from a fixed vocabulary (Gemma's has 256K of them). The tokenizer is Byte-Pair Encoding: it greedily merges the most frequent adjacent pairs until nothing else combines.

Why bother? Because it gives the model a stable vocabulary across languages and misspellings. "unhappiness" doesn't need its own entry; "un" + "happiness" covers it, and so does "un" + "happi" + "ness". Each token gets an integer ID (a number that points into the model's embedding table), and that's what actually flows downstream.

Subword tokenization is what makes these models multilingual without ballooning the vocabulary. Rare words become sequences of common pieces. Common words stay whole.

Why this matters

Your billable tokens start here. Every API charge, every context window limit, every latency estimate is denominated in tokens, not characters. Knowing that one "word" can equal four tokens explains a lot of weird pricing math.

C · Attention

Attention lets each token look at every other token.

Once you have a list of token vectors (each one a learned embedding of a chunk of text), the transformer needs a way for them to talk. That's attention. Every token produces three vectors from its embedding: a query (Q), a key (K), and a value (V).

To update itself, a token asks: how relevant is every other token to me? It scores each one by Q · K, softmaxes the scores into weights, and uses those weights to mix the V vectors. The result is a new vector that has been enriched with context from the whole sequence. Eight stacked layers in Gemma do this eight times, with a multi-layer perceptron in between.

The diagonal is heavy because the cat cares most about itself. Off-diagonal weights show which other tokens contribute to its new context vector. Each layer learns its own pattern.

Why this matters

Polysemy lives in attention. Without attention, bank and river bank get the same vector. With it, "river" can pull the meaning toward water by the time bank reaches its final layer. That's why transformer-era embeddings actually understand sentences, where earlier bag-of-words ones didn't.

D · Pooling

Many token vectors become one sentence vector.

After the last layer, every token has its own contextual vector. To turn the sequence into a single 768-dim vector, you pool. Gemma uses mean pooling: each dimension is averaged across all token positions, weighted by an attention mask so padding doesn't leak in.

Other models do CLS pooling (use the first token's vector) or last-token pooling. Mean is usually a safe default for retrieval because every input token gets a vote.

Every token votes equally. If your sentence is padded to a fixed length, mask the pads out so they don't dilute the average. The Hugging Face snippet for the Gemma embedding model does this by default.

Why this matters

How you pool shapes what similarity means. Mean-pool a sentence like "Not bad" and you get something close to "Okay". Last-token-pool it and you lean toward the trailing nuance. Pick the pooling that matches your retrieval question, not the one that's easiest to implement.

E · Cosine similarity

How close are two vectors? Use cosine.

Two vectors, a and b, point in roughly the same direction if the angle between them is small. Cosine similarity measures the cosine of that angle, giving a number in [-1, 1]. 1 means identical, 0 means orthogonal, -1 means opposite.

Why not Euclidean distance? Because embeddings live on or near a hypersphere. Two long documents and two short documents can have the same Euclidean distance with very different meanings. Cosine ignores magnitude, which is what you want when comparing directions of meaning rather than sizes.

The angle sweeps. The cosine readout updates. Watch what "close" really means: not closeness in space, but alignment in direction.

Why this matters

Retrieval runs on cosine. Pick the top-k nearest neighbors by cosine similarity over your corpus and you have a search engine. Cosine + a vector index (FAISS, ScaNN, Pinecone) is the whole recipe behind every modern RAG system.

F · Matryoshka (the Gemma twist)

Use the first 64 dims, or the first 768, your choice.

Standard embedding models force you to use the full 768 dimensions. Gemma is trained with Matryoshka Representation Learning: the model is taught to put the most useful signal in the first 64 dimensions, then layer the next 128, then 256, all the way to 768. You can truncate at any prefix and still get something useful.

Use 64 dims for cheap retrieval over millions of vectors. Use 768 for accurate reranking. The model is one checkpoint, and the engineering tradeoff is now yours.

Truncate anywhere. The first 64 dims are already useful because the model was trained to make them so. No retraining, no second embedding model.

Why this matters

Matryoshka changes your storage math. Indexing 100M chunks at 64 dims is roughly 8x cheaper than at 512. For most production retrieval workloads, the 256-dim prefix is the sweet spot between recall and cost.

D.2 · Pooling, compared

Three pooling strategies, three different sentences.

Same input, three poolers, three vectors. Mean pooling averages all token positions into one vector. CLS pooling grabs the special classification token's final vector. Last-token pooling grabs the final token, which under causal masking is the only token that has seen everything to its left.

The right one depends on your model. BERT-family models expect CLS pooling; decoder-only LLaMA-family models often prefer last-token. Gemma's own embedding model uses mean pooling, masked to ignore padding, which is what you get from the Hugging Face checkpoint by default.

Pooling is a model-family decision more than a personal preference. Match what the model was trained for, then your vectors behave like the paper says.

Why this matters

Wrong pooler throws away the model's signal. Running mean-pool over a decoder-only model that was fine-tuned with last-token pooling won't fail loudly; it will just quietly rank your documents worse. Always check the model's card or config before you wire it up.

F.2 · How it's trained

The geometry is learned, not given.

Embedding models are trained with contrastive learning: pairs of texts that should be near each other are pulled together, pairs that should be far apart are pushed apart. For Gemma, the positive pairs are paraphrases, queries with their answers, and similar documents mined from the training corpus. Negatives are sampled from the rest of the batch.

Each step of training is basically saying: "make these two sentences closer in vector space than these other ten." Repeat a trillion times and you have an embedding model that knows what humans mean by similar.

Each training step is a tiny rearrangement: positives slide inward, negatives slide outward. The trick is sampling enough hard negatives that the model doesn't get lazy.

Why this matters

Embedding quality rides on the data. Pretraining data, hard-negative mining, and the choice of contrastive loss are the levers that turn a generic transformer into a useful embedding model. Most "embedding quality" wins come from rebuilding the data pipeline, not the architecture.

Common confusions

Four things people get wrong about embeddings

Each card carries one common belief, then the version that holds up under pressure.

01

"Embeddings are the same thing as an LLM's internal hidden states, so we can just extract them from any model."

Gemma's embedding model is a separate checkpoint from the Gemma generative model. It's been fine-tuned specifically so the pooled vector has geometric meaning. Pulling hidden states out of a generative model gives you something, but it isn't designed to be retrieved against. The training objective is the difference.

02

"Bigger embeddings always beat smaller ones, so just use 3072 dims and you'll be fine."

Dim count is one knob among several. Larger dim means more storage, slower ANN search, and diminishing returns. For most retrieval problems, 256-768 is the practical sweet spot. Going higher helps mostly when the corpus is huge and the queries are very ambiguous.

03

"king - man + woman = queen, so embeddings encode literal analogical arithmetic."

The famous Word2Vec example works on a handful of vectors with a lot of cherry-picking. Modern transformer embeddings don't reliably do analogy by arithmetic. They're trained for similarity, not for symbolic manipulation. If you want reasoning, run a generative model on the retrieved context.

04

"A pretrained embedding model works for any domain out of the box."

Out-of-the-box Gemma embeddings are excellent for general English. For specialized domains (legal, medical, code, scientific literature), fine-tuning on a small in-domain contrastive dataset usually improves retrieval quality by 5-15%. The pretraining is a foundation, not a finished tool.
