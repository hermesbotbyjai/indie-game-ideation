Prefill Deflection: when a decode node steals prefill work.

In a disaggregated LLM serving stack, the bottleneck is rarely prefill compute. It is the queue and the KV-cache transfer. Kairos lets a decode node do both jobs at once, on its own terms, only when it pays off.

  • Why prefill compute is only 2 to 23 percent of P95 TTFT
  • How a decode node has room to host prefill chunks
  • The TBT-safe chunk sweep that makes deflection safe
  • How the paper got 81 percent P95 TTFT cuts on a 2P2D cluster
The model in one sentence

Kairos picks the prefill phase of a request up off a saturated prefill node, runs it on a decode node as small chunked-prefill steps that share the decoder's batch, and only when the predicted new TTFT is below the old one and no in-flight decoder's Time-Between-Tokens breaks its SLO.

The mental model

Two pools, one asymmetric bottleneck, one rerouting knob.

The walkthrough

Four ideas, each with one paragraph, one diagram, one reason it matters.

Disaggregation created a new asymmetry, it did not fix the old one. A modern serving stack runs two separate GPU pools: a prefill pool for prompt processing, a decode pool for token generation. The prefill node produces the KV cache and ships it across the network to the decode node, which then generates the response one token at a time.[1][3] The headline idea is that prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, so each phase gets the hardware that suits it.

The trouble is that under bursty, heavy-tailed prompt distributions, the prefill node becomes the bottleneck and decode nodes sit mostly idle. The Microsoft team measured a 2P2D A100 cluster across five production-style traces: prefill SM utilization was 75 to 84 percent, decode SM utilization was 12 to 27 percent. The two pools are not symmetric, the spare capacity lives on the wrong side of the wire.

Why this matters

If you are sizing clusters, the prefill pool is the one that grows first and hurts first. Adding more decode nodes does not help, because the request never reaches them. This is the core tension Kairos tries to relax.

P95 TTFT is not bottlenecked on prefill compute, it is bottlenecked on queue plus KV transfer. Across all five traces the authors ran, actual prefill execution time was only 2 to 23 percent of P95 Time-to-First-Token. The remaining 77 to 98 percent was prefill queue wait, plus the inter-node GPU-to-GPU KV cache transfer that has to happen before the first decode step can start.[1] Under bursty arrival, the queue can spike to 840 ms and the KV transfer to 2.1 seconds at P95.

So the obvious lever is not "make prefill faster." The compute is fine. The lever is "let prefill happen somewhere that already has KV transfer built in." That is a decode node, which already owns the request's KV cache once generation starts. If the prefill of a new request is run in place on a decode node, the transfer step vanishes.

Why this matters

Most inference engineering focuses on prefill kernel speed, KV cache layout, or tokenizer latency. Those are 2 to 23 percent of the problem. The real cost is the scheduling fabric between the two phases. Any time spent shaving a millisecond off the GEMM is invisible if the request is sitting in a 2-second queue.

Deflection only works if it does not break the in-flight decoders, and that is the whole game. Kairos is not "always deflect, hope for the best." For every queued request, the dispatcher first estimates its TTFT on the prefill node as the queue wait plus the prefill compute. Then, for every decode node, it sweeps over candidate chunk size sequences and asks two questions. Question one: would adding this chunked prefill to the decode node's batch push any in-flight decoder's Time-Between-Tokens past its SLO? Question two: is the projected TTFT on the decode node less than alpha times the projected TTFT on the prefill node?[1] Only if both pass does Kairos deflect.

The chunk size matters. The paper's profiling shows that for chunk sizes up to 512 tokens, adding a prefill chunk to a decode batch increases the decode step latency by at most 30 ms across the tested workloads. Above that, the step latency can grow enough to break TBT. The dispatcher's job is to find the largest safe chunk that still wins on TTFT, and run that. The per-request decision takes under 1 millisecond, so the dispatch loop itself is not the bottleneck.[1]

Why this matters

The reason "just co-locate prefill and decode" sounds free is that you skip the KV transfer. The reason nobody does it that way is that co-location breaks TBT under burst. Kairos gets the best of both by borrowing prefill work in small, schedule-checked slices. That is also why the safety check, not the routing trick, is the load-bearing piece.

The numbers come from production-shaped traces, not synthetic benchmarks. The evaluation uses a 2 prefill, 2 decode A100 cluster, DeepSeek-V2-Lite as the model, and a trace from "Company X" that the authors describe as production derived. Across that setup, the static baselines collapse in obvious ways. A queue-length threshold that only deflects when the prefill queue is fully empty under-deflects and gets nothing. A prompt-length threshold that always deflects short requests over-deflects and breaks TBT 1.5 percent of the time. The Oracle that knows the future and sweeps every possible schedule lands at 2.6 second P95 TTFT and 99.5 percent SLO attainment.[1]

Kairos is a deployable approximation of the Oracle. The published numbers are P95 TTFT reductions up to 81 percent and SLO attainment improvements up to 79 percent over the state-of-the-art disaggregated schedulers it was compared against, with a per-request routing cost of under one millisecond. The implementation is roughly 2,000 lines of Python on top of vLLM 0.18.1.[1] That last detail is the part an architect cares about: a research paper that ships as a vLLM patch is much more likely to land in your stack than one that ships as a paper.

Why this matters

Most LLM serving "breakthroughs" that get HN points are wall-clock wins on toy benchmarks. The interesting question is always: does this survive your real request mix, and can you actually deploy it. The Kairos numbers above are both. If you are running vLLM in production today, you can read the patch.

Common confusions

Four things people get wrong about prefill deflection.

Confusion 01

"Prefill deflection is just disaggregated prefill with the wires crossed."

It looks like that, but the wiring is exactly the same. The dispatcher picks a destination. What changes is the policy. In classical PD disaggregation, every prefill goes to a prefill node. In Kairos, some prefills go to a decode node, executed in chunks that share the decode batch. The KV transfer between pools is the cost you are trying to avoid, and deflection deletes it for the requests that need it most.[1][3]

Confusion 02

"This is just chunked prefill, like Sarathi."

Chunked prefill is the mechanism. Kairos borrows it. The difference is that Sarathi uses a fixed chunk size and runs prefill and decode co-located on the same node, with the constant tension between TTFT and TBT. Kairos keeps disaggregation and chooses, per request, a chunk size that fits the current decode batch on the target decode node. The chunk sweep is what sets it apart from a Sarathi-style colocation.[1][4]

Confusion 03

"Deflection will break TBT the moment load spikes, so the safety check is theater."

The whole paper is the safety check. The dispatcher computes, before routing, the largest chunk size sequence on each decode node that would keep every in-flight decoder's TBT under the SLO. If no such schedule exists on any decode node, the request goes to a prefill node and nothing changes. The under-deflecting baselines (SQ with threshold 4) sit at zero deflections, the over-deflecting one (SL with prompt < 2048) breaks TBT 1.5 percent of the time, and Kairos lands at zero violations because it refuses to deflect unless the safety check passes.[1]

Confusion 04

"This is the same trick as PPD multi-turn deflection."

It is adjacent, not identical. PPD (Prefill-Prefill-capable Decode) also routes work to a decode node, but only for Turn 2+ requests, on the rationale that append-prefill with cached KV is much cheaper than full prefill and only mildly disturbs decode. Kairos is more general: any request can be deflected if the decode node has headroom and the safety check passes. PPD is a special case of the same idea, restricted to multi-turn traffic and a fixed heuristic.[1]
