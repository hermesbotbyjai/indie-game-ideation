Day 13 · Decision models

Microsoft-Decision-1: the model that scores instead of chats.

A small, post-trained 9B model that takes a fixed list of answers and returns a calibrated probability for each one. The point isn't to write better prose. The point is to be 35× faster than GPT-6 Sol at the boring, structured calls every AI agent already makes a hundred times per request.

The model in one sentence
Microsoft-Decision-1 takes any "pick from a fixed set of options" prompt, runs a single forward pass on a post-trained Qwen3.5-9B, and returns a calibrated probability for every option, so the routing, classification, and grading calls inside your agents stop paying the latency and cost of a chat-tuned LLM.

The mental model.

Two systems, one problem. Most AI applications in 2026 route every small judgment through a System Two model that was trained to chat and reason. Microsoft-Decision-1 is the System One model: it doesn't reason, it scores. Once you see the split, the speed and cost claims fall out for free.

The walkthrough.

Five things you need to know to actually use this thing, in the order you'll hit them.

1. "Decision model" is a category, not a Microsoft invention.

The blog post calls Decision-1 the first of a new category. It isn't. The category has existed since at least H2O's decision-scoring work and got formalized as "System One" thinking earlier in 2026 by TypeSafe AI with their Jev model. What Microsoft did is take a serious open-weight base (Qwen3.5-9B), post-train it for one structured job, and ship it inside a Foundry endpoint that enterprise procurement already trusts.

The naming war matters less than the shift. Three vendors now compete on the same shape of API: hand the model a list, get probabilities back, do the routing in your own code.

Why this matters When a category gets a third serious entrant with Microsoft distribution, the LLM-as-router pattern starts to look like a planning bug. Most teams in 2026 still pay chat-model prices for routing calls. That's no longer the only option, or even the cheapest one.

2. The output is a probability vector, not text.

Decision-1 takes a structured API call, a prompt, and a list of allowed answers like "yes / no", "urgent / normal / low", "tool_a / tool_b / tool_c", or a rubric for grading an AI response. It returns one calibrated probability per option. The JSON you get back is already a routing decision. Your code can act on it without an extra parse step, without regex, and without a second model validating the first one.

Calibrated is the load-bearing word. The probabilities track real frequencies, so a 0.73 isn't a vibe, it's a guess you can threshold or average over a batch.

Why this matters Every LLM-as-classifier hack you've ever shipped (parse the verb, regex the JSON, retry on malformed output) exists to work around the fact that chat models emit prose. With calibrated probabilities, you can delete the parser.

3. It's a 9B model post-trained on top of Qwen3.5-9B.

Decision-1 isn't a from-scratch architecture or even a from-scratch pretraining run. Microsoft took Qwen3.5-9B, an open-weight 9-billion-parameter base, and post-trained it for the structured-output head. The roadmap explicitly says the same trick will be re-applied on Microsoft AI's own models and on OpenAI base models.

This is the part that quietly undercuts the "Microsoft research breakthrough" framing. The architecture is Alibaba's. The training data and the evaluation harness are Microsoft's. The win is the recipe, not the substrate.

Why this matters For you as an architect: post-training a small open-weight base for a narrow job is the dominant cost pattern for the next two years. Decision-1 is the textbook example, not an exception.

4. The speed claim is a single-pass claim.

Decision-1 doesn't reason through the decision, then summarize the reasoning. There is no chain-of-thought. There is one forward pass through the 9B base, one softmax over the option logits, one JSON response. Microsoft measures it 4.5× faster than Quyet-1.0-Large (the runner-up at the same job) and 35× faster than GPT-6 Sol.

The H2O-Lightning-4B number is interesting on a different level. The runner-up is 4B. A 4B reasoning-tuned classifier got beat by a 9B post-trained non-reasoner. You can read that as "scale always wins" or as "reasoning was load-bearing only because no one had trained a non-reasoner for this job yet." Microsoft bet on the second reading, and the bet is paying.

Why this matters If your agent does ten classification calls per turn, dropping from 2.0 s to 55 ms each turns a 20-second turn into a 0.5-second turn. Latency budget is the thing that gates agent UX, and this changes it.

5. Cost: $0.042 per million input tokens, output free.

Pricing is the second half of the pitch. Output tokens are free, which is the right shape for a model whose output is a JSON object three floats long. Per call, on the average 1K-token agent routing prompt, Decision-1 is roughly two orders of magnitude cheaper than GPT-6 Sol and several times cheaper than H2O-Lightning-4B.

But the interesting bit is the volume effect. The Xbox Research team used Decision-1 to sort 10K pieces of open-ended player feedback into themes, and reported quality competitive with GPT-5 at 80 to 100× the speed. That's a workload nobody was doing with a frontier model because the unit economics didn't work. Decision-1 makes the workload economic.

Why this matters Pricing changes what your team is allowed to ship. A 100x cost drop turns "we'll only run the grader on the top 5% of candidates" into "we'll grade every candidate, every time." That unlocks whole product behaviors that weren't cost-effective before.

Common confusions.

Things people keep getting wrong in the first hour of looking at this. Get them out of the way.

01

"It's a smaller, faster LLM."

No. It is not chat-tuned and you should not prompt it like one.

Decision-1 has no instruction-following surface for free text. Give it a list of options and a request, get a probability vector back. If you ask it a question it can't score, it'll give you nonsense with confidence. Treat it like a logistic regression on a transformer substrate, not like a chat model that happens to be small.

02

"It's just a classifier."

Not in the scikit-learn sense, and that's the point.

A 2024 classifier was a single-label argmax with no calibrated probabilities. Decision-1 returns a full softmax that's calibrated against held-out benchmarks, supports open-ended option vocabularies per call, and accepts rubric-based grading of AI responses. That's a different shape than fit-predict on a CSV.

03

"Microsoft built the model."

The base is Alibaba's Qwen3.5-9B. Microsoft's contribution is the post-training.

Microsoft is open about this: the model is post-trained on Qwen3.5-9B, with future rebases planned on Microsoft AI and OpenAI bases. The vendor lock-in question for Decision-1 is mostly about the evaluation harness and the rubric schema, not the weights.

04

"It replaces my LLM."

It replaces. This is a routing model, not a reasoning model.

Decision-1 doesn't know how to summarize a document, draft an email, or walk through a multi-step plan. What it does is tell you, fast and cheaply, which of three tools your agent should call next. You still need System Two for the parts that need prose or planning. You just stop paying System Two prices for the parts that don't.
