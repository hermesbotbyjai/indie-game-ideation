Day 25 · Inference Systems

DeepInfer and the split that decides everything

DeepSeek open-sourced the engine behind its production serving stack. Reading it well means understanding one distinction that explains every optimization inside it.

  • Why prefill and decode are different problems with different bottlenecks, and why that forces different hardware.
  • How a mixture-of-experts model moves tokens across 64 to 128 GPUs without the slowest one stalling everyone.
  • Why speculative decoding is load-dependent, not a free 2x, with real production numbers.
  • What "offline engine" actually means and which three pieces are conspicuously absent.
The model in one sentence

Every DeepInfer optimization is an answer to one question: is this step compute-bound (prefill) or memory-bound (decode)?

The mental model

One request, two completely different bottlenecks

When you send a prompt, the model does two kinds of work. The first reads your prompt all at once and is limited by how fast the GPU can multiply. The second writes one token at a time and is limited by how fast the GPU can read weights. Everything else is downstream of that.

Same model, same GPUs. The bottleneck flips between the two phases, which is why they end up on different hardware.

Walkthrough

Five decisions that follow from the split

Each of these is a direct consequence of the prefill/decode distinction. Read them in order and the design starts to feel inevitable rather than clever.

01 · The split itself

Prefill and decode get separate hardware pools

In DeepSeek's production layout, prefill runs on 8-GPU machines with expert parallelism of 2 to 8. Decode runs on 64 to 128 GPU supernodes with expert parallelism of 64 to 128. Same model, same weights, radically different cluster shapes.

The reason is the split from the diagram. Prefill is compute-bound, so you want the smallest configuration that fits the model in HBM without wasting arithmetic units. Decode is bandwidth-bound, so you want maximum aggregate memory bandwidth, which means spreading the work across as many GPUs as the interconnect allows.

The cost is that now you have to move KV cache between two pools and balance two workloads with opposite shapes. DeepSeek's own guidance is that the smallest efficient unit is 64 prefill GPUs plus 64 decode GPUs, so a small cluster will not see the same efficiency as a large one.

Same weights, opposite bottlenecks. Aggregate bandwidth is why decode fans out wide.

Why this matters

When you read any serving benchmark, the first question is which phase it measured and on what cluster shape. A prefill number on 4 GPUs and a decode number on 64 GPUs are not comparable, and DeepSeek's published charts use exactly that split. Most "we got 10x throughput" claims are really "we changed the cluster shape".

02 · MoE movement

Why Wide-EP is the answer to bandwidth, not compute

In a mixture-of-experts model, every token picks a few experts out of many, and those experts live on specific GPUs. To compute a token, you have to ship the token to those GPUs and ship the results back. That is an all-to-all communication pattern, and at 128 GPUs it is genuinely expensive.

The naive fix is to pack related experts onto the same GPU to reduce shipping. That works until routing gets uneven: some experts become hot, and one GPU becomes the slowest while 127 sit idle. That is why DeepSeek's EPLB keeps redundant copies of hot experts and periodically reshuffles assignments based on a window of recent load.

The number to watch is moe_lb, the slowest rank divided by the mean. At decode scale DeepSeek reports 1.090, meaning the slowest GPU does about 9% more work than average. Everyone waits at the barrier, so that 9% is 9% of everyone's throughput.

Replication costs memory. It buys you a barrier that does not stall. At 128 GPUs that trade is worth it.

Why this matters

MoE models are sold on parameter count but paid for on communication. If you are evaluating whether an MoE deployment will scale, the question is not "how big is the model" but "what is your slowest rank doing while everything else idles". That ratio is the real capacity ceiling.

03 · Speculation

Speculative decoding is a load-dependent trade

The idea is straightforward: a cheap draft model proposes several tokens ahead, then the real model verifies all of them in one forward pass. If the draft was right, you got several tokens for the cost of one. If it was wrong, you fall back to one.

DSpark's contribution is deciding how many drafts to verify. A confidence head predicts the acceptance probability of each proposed token, and the scheduler verifies only the prefix worth verifying. Fixed-length verification does wasted work when the draft model is likely to be wrong.

Here is the part worth remembering. At DeepSeek's production operating point of about 60 sequences per rank, dynamic length gives 4 to 7% throughput. At 96 sequences per rank it gives 11%. At 16 sequences per rank it is a 1.5% loss. Verification work drops roughly 30% while accepted length barely moves. This is not a free lunch, it is a load-dependent optimization with a crossover point.

Verified drafts drop from 5.00 to 3.2-4.4. Accepted length barely moves. The gain only shows up under load.

Why this matters

Any optimization that adds work per step needs enough concurrent work to amortize it. Speculation is a bandwidth-bound-phase optimization, so its payoff scales with batch size. If you are running low-concurrency interactive traffic, expect the naive version to be fine and the clever version to cost you 1.5%.

04 · Approximation

Bounded replay: making a cache miss cheap instead of storing everything

Sliding-window attention normally needs the last N tokens of KV cache at every layer. The naive move is to keep a cache of that window per request. The problem is memory: with many concurrent requests and many layers, that window becomes a large fraction of your HBM budget.

Bounded replay takes the opposite approach. Keep almost nothing. When attention needs the window, rebuild it by replaying just the last 128 tokens, with position i attending only to [max(s, i-127), i]. The release's default keeps a 140-token suffix, the extra 12 covering speculative backtracking.

This is deliberately approximate and DeepSeek says so. It is not bit-exact. vLLM independently implemented the same idea on GSM8K and GPQA and measured gaps within about 1.5 standard errors, which is evidence the approach is sound. It is not validation of DeepInfer's specific code.

Trading a bit of compute for a lot of memory. The approximation is the product, not a bug.

Why this matters

There are two ways to fit in a memory budget: shrink the model or shrink the per-request state. Bounded replay attacks the second. It is a good example of a co-design decision: the model architecture and the engine were tuned together, so the window size and the replay length are not independent knobs.

05 · Overlap

The gap between decode steps is where throughput leaks

A naive decode step is schedule → forward → sample → post-process. The first and last steps run on the CPU. Before post-process can finish, the CPU needs the sampling result back from the GPU. So the GPU sits idle while the CPU processes that result and builds the next batch.

DeepInfer overlaps the two streams so the CPU prepares batch N+1 while the GPU is still running batch N. Async scheduling is on by default. The idea is not new (DeepSeek credits SGLang and NanoFlow), but having it in the release with correct synchronization is the difference between a fast kernel and a serving engine.

There is a less glamorous feature in the same spirit: batch-invariant inference. Dynamic batching changes the order of floating-point reductions, so the same prompt can produce slightly different logits depending on what else was in the batch. For chat nobody notices. For benchmarking, auditing, or reproducing an agent run, it makes results unauditable. Setting DEEPINFER_BATCH_INVARIANT=1 makes the same prompt return the same logits regardless of batch composition.

Async scheduling is enabled by default. It removes idle time rather than making the GPU faster.

Why this matters

Batch-invariance is the one to steal even if you never run DeepInfer. If your agent evals are noisy across runs and you cannot tell whether a regression came from a model change or from which requests happened to batch together, you cannot debug it. Paying for determinism at inference is a real operational option that most frameworks leave out.

The boundary

Engine, not service

The README is unusually direct about what is absent. DeepInfer is an offline batch engine. There is no HTTP front end, no KV cache system, no prefill/decode disaggregation, and no load balancing across data-parallel replicas.

LayerStatusWhat you would still need
Model runtime IN Nothing. V4.1-Flash forward pass and sampler.
Optimized kernels IN Nothing. FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, DeepEP, TileKernels, DeepSelect.
Parallelism IN Nothing. Attention DP, context parallelism, MoE expert parallelism and load balancing.
KV cache system OUT A prefix cache that survives across requests and machines.
Prefill/decode split OUT Separate pools plus cache transfer and routing between them.
Load balancing OUT Routing aware of both request count and cache length.
Online API OUT Auth, quotas, billing, observability. The separate deepseek-recipe repo handles protocol conversion.

This is a design boundary, not a defect. The missing pieces are the ones most tightly coupled to DeepSeek's internal infrastructure, so it is unlikely they ever ship. But it changes who can use this: you can run batch jobs over a corpus, you cannot put it in front of users without building a serving layer first.

The gap is honest and load-bearing. Understanding where it sits tells you what the release is actually for.

Common confusions

Four things people get wrong about this release

01
"DeepInfer is an OpenAI-compatible server."
It is a drop-in replacement for vLLM that you point users at.
It is an offline batch engine for one model (V4.1-Flash) on SM100 or Ascend 950 hardware. No HTTP layer, one supported checkpoint. If you want an API today, DeepSeek shipped deepseek-recipe as a separate repo, and it is the piece that makes protocol compatibility work.
02
"Speculative decoding makes inference 2x faster."
Draft tokens are basically always right, so you skip the model.
It is load-dependent. DeepSeek's own numbers: +4 to 7% at ~60 sequences per rank, +11% at 96, and -1.5% at 16. The mechanism cuts verification work roughly 30% while accepted length barely changes. Below the crossover point you are paying for the draft model and getting nothing back.
03
"The production numbers apply to the open-source release."
The EPD deployment, Wide-EP at 128 GPUs, and the 100 PiB KV cache all ship in the repo.
The README lists three things as not included: the KV cache system, prefill/decode disaggregation, and data-parallel load balancing. The production design is documented so you understand the intent. It is not a feature list for the repository.
04
"The benchmark charts mean the model is fast."
2318 points on HN and 18.9k GitHub stars, so the numbers must be validated.
As of the release snapshot there were zero third-party reproductions. The decode chart assumes a 75% draft-acceptance rate and uses nextn=3 fixed, which is not the default deployment config. Context is 131,072, not the model's 1M maximum. Read the axes before the headline.
