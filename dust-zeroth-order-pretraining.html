Dust: pretraining transformers without backprop, by treating each token as a population member.

A new method drops gradient computation, replaces it with a reward signal on perturbed activations, and still keeps up with backprop on transformer pretraining. The trick is parallelism along the token axis, not the batch axis.

  • Why backprop is the credit-assignment monopoly you forgot was a monopoly
  • How Dust makes a forward pass into thousands of parallel ES members
  • Why a 243M model is more population-efficient than a 37M model, the opposite of the usual intuition
The model in one sentence

Dust trains transformer weights by adding per-token Gaussian noise to every linear layer's output, scoring each token's noise by the change in its token loss, and averaging reward-weighted perturbations across the sequence so one forward pass stands in as a virtual ES batch of thousands of members.

The mental model

Population goes along the token axis, gradient is recovered from a free batch axis.

Backprop computes the analytic gradient from the chain rule. Dust throws that away and uses search instead. The trick is what the search space is built out of.

The walkthrough

Five moves, each with one paragraph, one diagram, one reason it matters.

Backprop is the credit-assignment monopoly you forgot was a monopoly. Every modern transformer is trained by running a forward pass, computing a scalar loss, then running a backward pass that propagates that loss backward through the chain rule. The backward pass is what makes training tractable at scale, and almost every optimization you know about, Adam, Lion, learning-rate schedules, weight decay, is built around the assumption that you have an analytic per-parameter gradient to apply it to.[1]

Dust asks a different question. What if you threw the gradient away and replaced it with a search? The authors are explicit about the framing. Modern architectures, optimizers, and hardware have grown up around backprop. They are not trying to dethrone it. They are showing that in a compute-rich regime, where you can afford a few hundred extra forward passes per parameter update, search-based credit assignment is in the same league, and in some settings wins.[1][2]

Why this matters

If you have ever wondered whether the entire modern deep learning stack is an accident of one algorithm, this paper is the first clean evidence that the answer is no, the search alternative is viable. It is also evidence that backprop's monopoly is not a law of nature.

The virtual population is the trick that makes the cost work. Classical evolution strategies perturb the weights: you draw N noise vectors, materialize N copies of the model, run a forward pass on each, and average the reward-weighted perturbations to get a gradient estimate. The compute is brutal, one member costs one forward pass, and you need thousands of members for the estimate to converge.[2]

Dust sidesteps that by perturbing activations, not weights. Adding Gaussian noise to the output of a linear layer at every token is equivalent, in expectation, to a low-rank perturbation of the weight matrix, except you never copy it. Because each token in a transformer sequence carries its own independent noise, one forward pass evaluates one ES member per token. A modern training sequence of 4,096 tokens becomes a population of 4,096 members per pass, for free, on the same hardware that runs one forward pass for backprop.[1]

Why this matters

The trade-off here is not "no compute for faster than backprop." It is "use a much larger population, evaluate all of it in parallel along the token axis." When the population is large enough, the search beats the analytic gradient. The same memory budget, no backward pass stored, but a lot more forward work.

The gradient comes from the outer product, and the outer product comes from the reward-weighted noise. Here is the full algorithm in one paragraph. Forward: at every linear layer, add Gaussian noise epsilon_t to the output activation y_t, independently at every token. Backward is skipped. Score: at each token, the reward is the change in that token's loss caused by epsilon_t. Average: the reward-weighted average of epsilon_t across all tokens is an estimate of the error signal at the layer's output. The weight update is the outer product of that error estimate with the layer's input.[1]

This is node perturbation, an old idea from Widrow and Lehr's 1990 review, Werfel, Xie, and Seung's 2003 paper, and Le Cun, Galland, and Hinton's 1988 GEMINI work. The novelty in Dust is the per-token virtual population, the way it composes with modern transformer shapes, and the explicit handling of interference between perturbed modules (cache the clean forward, only rerun from block l onward when sampling block l's draws).[1][3][4]

Why this matters

If you have ever wondered how the gradient could possibly come out of forward-only computation, the outer-product identity is the answer. The reward-weighted noise is a Monte Carlo estimate of the gradient with respect to the layer's input, and the outer product lifts that to the weight matrix. The estimate has the right expectation, but higher variance than backprop's analytic gradient.

Attention is where naive node perturbation breaks. If you add Gaussian noise to the attention internals (the Q, K, V, gate, and value embedding) at every token and reward each token by its own loss delta, the noise barely registers. The reason is that attention is a softmax over future tokens, so perturbing one token's Q vector affects many tokens' outputs and rewards, in a way the token-local reward cannot disentangle.[1]

Dust handles attention internals with a variant of the same recipe: instead of crediting the perturbation through the token's own loss, credit it through the estimated error at the attention output over current and future tokens. This is still forward-only, still uses the same outer-product form, but the credit assignment signal is taken from a downstream error estimate rather than a local reward. The result is that attention trains roughly as well as it does under backprop, despite the search-only objective.[1]

Why this matters

The asymmetry between MLP and attention credit assignment is the part that makes Dust not just "noise on activations, done." If you forget to switch the reward signal for attention, you train a model that learns nothing useful at the attention layers. The 2L residual mixing scalars still get ordinary weight-space ES, because there are only 2L of them and the overhead is negligible.

Larger models are more population-efficient, the opposite of the usual intuition. Zeroth-order methods are widely believed not to scale. The classic curse of dimensionality argument is that adding noise to a high-dimensional weight vector loses all direction, so the gradient estimate is swamped by noise. Larger models have larger weight tensors, so the intuition says they should be worse, not better.[1][2]

Dust inverts this. The model-size study in the appendix compares 0.3M, 3.8M, 37.7M, and 243M parameter variants of the same architecture. At every population size tested, the 243M model achieves a lower test loss than the 37.7M model. The authors read this as a new view of overparameterization: the loss landscape is smoother in higher dimensions, so a fixed population budget gets further. The cosine similarity between Dust's estimated gradient and backprop's analytic gradient on the same batch climbs from noise at small populations to 0.6 to 0.9 alignment at large populations, on every layer type, at every scale tested up to 1B tokens.[1]

Why this matters

This is the part that, if it replicates, might be the start of a real alternative to backprop. If larger zeroth-order methods are not just viable but more sample-efficient per parameter, the entire credit-assignment monopoly of backprop is on borrowed time. The authors are careful not to claim this, but the appendix tables do most of the work.

Common confusions

Four things people get wrong about zeroth-order pretraining.

Confusion 01

"Dust means no compute and no memory. It's free."

The opposite. Dust skips the backward pass, but it uses more forward passes. The authors are explicit: Dust does not aim to be compute-efficient enough to replace backprop today, and the wins over backprop at 10M and 20M tokens come at a population that costs substantially more compute. What you save is the backward graph (no activations stored for the backward) and you trade memory for forward FLOPs.[1]

Confusion 02

"Per-token independent noise means no signal at long context."

It does mean no token-to-token gradient through the noise itself, which is exactly why Dust uses a different credit rule for attention internals (credit them via the estimated error at the attention output, over current and future tokens). The MLP and value embeddings can use the local per-token reward, attention cannot. The asymmetry is explicit in the paper and is the part that is hardest to get right in an implementation.[1]

Confusion 03

"Larger models = harder for zeroth-order methods."

The opposite, and this is the most surprising result. A 243M model achieves lower test loss than a 37.7M model at every population size tested, and a 37.7M model beats the smaller ones at every population. The authors read this as: more parameters creates a more benign search landscape, so the fixed population budget gets further. Gradient estimates align better with backprop at larger populations and at larger models, so the two effects compound.[1]

Confusion 04

"Dust still uses backprop somewhere."

Not in the weights you care about. Every linear in the residual path is trained by Dust's reward-weighted noise. The 2L residual mixing scalars are still trained by ordinary weight-space ES, because there are only 2L of them and materializing them as a separate batch axis population is cheap. The architecture is fully forward-only at the weight level, with no analytic gradient anywhere in the residual stream.[1]

Where to go next

Three followups that earn their click.

The paper

Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation

Dahal, Mandal, Gülbahar, Vegesna, October 2026. Read section 2 for the method, section 4 for the overparameterization experiments, and the appendix E for population allocation. The paper is unusually clear about what it does and does not claim.

 The implementation

qlabs-eng/dust on GitHub

A modded-nanoGPT training loop with Dust's reward-weighted noise step, Adam variant, and the four-population ladder from the main table. The reward routing for attention vs MLP is the most useful file to read in isolation.

 The baseline to know first

EGGROLL: Evolution Strategies at the Hyperscale

Sarkar et al., November 2025. The weight-space ES method Dust compares against. Read this first if you want to understand why Dust's 10^3 to 10^4x efficiency claim is measured against the right opponent. Low-rank perturbations make EGGROLL viable at large population, but each member is still one batch slot.
